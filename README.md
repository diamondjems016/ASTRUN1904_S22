This repository contains materials for ASTUN1904, a non-major astronomy lab at Columbia University, that was taught in Spring 2022.  Labs were run once per week for 3 hours at night.  There is intended to be minimal to no work outside of the lab. 
This lab focused on phenomena beyond the Solar System, including but not limited to exoplanets, dark matter, stellar spectroscopy, and Hubble expansion.
The particular section of the lab I taught had 6 students, making it easy to solicit and incorporate student feedback.
The second time I taught the lab in Spring 2023 (materials in public repo ASTRUN1904_S23), reflects some of that feedback.

Syllabus:
The first section is devoted to the class overview, which makes clear the learning objectives of
the class as well as a quick breakdown of the class structure. Doing this makes the class
expectations easy to find for the students.
This is followed by details on the materials that students are expected to bring to class. This
helps to ensure that students are at least materially prepared to participate in the class. We
also are sure to make clear the materials that may be difficult financially for students to obtain
(i.e. laptops) are available to them for the class.
The grading breakdown sets clear expectations for how students should expect to be evaluated
in the class. Of particular note is the participation section. In the past, the participation section
was, for lack of a better description, a fudge factor, for which there were no clear guidelines for
how to do well beyond “ask questions and participate in class”. In this updated syllabus,
multiple avenues for engagement with the class were provided to ensure that students were
able to participate in a way they were comfortable with.
Finally, we included policies for attendance, accommodations, and academic honesty to make
clear to students what would and would not be tolerated in the class, and what steps they need
to take for exceptions to the policies listed in the document.

Labs:
Labs for this course was designed by myself and Ryan Golant.  Each lab (1-10) is intended to be a self-contained lesson and exploration of that week's concept.  While the lab nominally touches on topics relevant to several astronomy lecture classes that link to the lab, since there are multiple lecture classes that link to the lab, it is not an exact match with respect to topics or timing of topics.  These labs were designed with the intention of having students explore each topic using several modes of engagement including interactive web applications, simulations, data, interacting with domain experts, and physical modeling. Each lab ends with the same section: a space for students to voice remaining questions, and a space for students to provide feedback on the lab.  While students often expressed interest in the lab material and the modes of engagement, the labs were frequently cited as too long for the time period allotted for the lab.  As each of these were new labs and being tested during this course, although adjustments were made to future labs, it was often difficult to tell ahead of time if the lab was well-suited for the time period allotted.  Adjustments to each lab were made for the second iteration of this course in Spring 2023 and these labs can be found in my public ASTRUN1904_S23 repository.

Final Project:
For this iteration of the course, the final project was issued as a standard slide presentation on a topic of their choice.  While the choice of topic gave students agency in their own learning, an improved version of the project that enabled students to also choose their mode of presentation was run in Spring 2023. For this iteration of the lab, students were encouraged to choose a topic that had not been covered in class at all, and were provided guidance about major questions they might consider answering about their chosen topic.  The rubric was provided upfront so that students could reference it as they crafted their presentations.  This rubric is the same as the one used in the Chambliss competition run at each meeting of the American Astronomical Society.
